# D.1 — Equity Research: The Person Behind "Buy, Target ₹2,450"

**The job in one line:** follow a small set of companies so closely that investors pay for your opinion on what they are worth.

**The people:** a *sell-side* research team. One lead analyst (their name goes on the report). One or two associates (that's you, for the first few years). Together they cover 10–20 companies in one sector. This is called the "coverage universe." Their readers are *buy-side* investors: mutual funds, insurance companies, foreign investors. There are also *buy-side analysts*. They do the same work, but privately, for their own fund. Nothing gets published.

## The process — how a research note gets made

**Station 1 · Own the model.** Every covered company has a living Excel model. It holds the quarterly profit and loss, balance sheet, and cash flow. Below those sit the *drivers*. For a retailer: store count × revenue per store. You know this already — it is MoneyMart's decomposition from notebook 5A, done at industrial scale. The associate's first real job is to maintain these models. Every quarter, new actual numbers go in. Every assumption gets questioned again.

**Station 2 · The quarter loop (results season).** Four times a year, everything speeds up:
1. **Preview** (one week before results): publish what you expect — revenue, EBITDA, profit — and what you will watch for.
2. **Results day**: the company files its numbers at, say, 5:30 pm. You have *hours*, not days. Put the actuals into the model. Compare them with your estimates. Join the management call at 6:30 and type notes. Then **the results note goes out that night**: "Revenue 3% above our estimate. Margins missed by 0.8% because input costs rose. We cut our FY26 profit estimate by 4%. We keep our BUY rating. Target price moves from ₹2,450 to ₹2,380."
3. **Re-model**: over the next two days, update the full model properly.
This loop is why research associates talk about results season the way students talk about exam week. It comes four times a year, forever.

**Station 3 · The channel check.** Between quarters comes the work that sets analysts apart. Talk to dealers, distributors, suppliers, and competitors. These are called "channels." You learn things before the company announces them. "Are car dealers giving bigger discounts this month?" — the filings do not have this yet. One strict rule: using secret company information is illegal. The skill is building a picture from public pieces and channel conversations. This is called the "mosaic."

**Station 4 · The valuation and the call.** The model produces forecasts. The forecasts feed a valuation. Usually **both** methods are used: a DCF and comparison multiples. Your 8B football field is exactly this step. The valuation gives a **target price**. Target versus current price gives the **rating**: Buy, Hold, or Sell. One honest fact to know early: sell-side ratings lean positive. Companies give access to analysts who are kind to them. An analyst's credibility comes from the courage to publish a painful Sell sometimes.

**Station 5 · The two big documents.**
- **Initiation note** (30–60 pages, weeks of work): starting coverage on a new company. Industry structure, business model, full model, valuation, risks. The associate builds most of it. It is the research version of your capstone.
- **Results and update notes** (2–6 pages, hours of work): the routine output. The shape: your view first → what changed → the revised numbers table → valuation → risks. Notice the pattern. **Answer first, evidence below. Module 12.5's pyramid is the standard here.**

**Station 6 · Talking to clients.** Notes go to clients. Then the analyst *talks*: client calls, roadshows, and arranging meetings between fund managers and company management. The buy-side then votes on which analysts were useful. Those votes decide where the buy-side sends its business. That vote — not the report PDF — is the real product.

## A Tuesday in results season (associate, consumer sector)

7:15 read overnight foreign-investor flows and the sector chat groups · 8:00 today a company reports: check final estimates, re-read the preview note · 9:15 market opens, watch your companies · 11:00 update the channel-check tracker from yesterday's distributor calls · 14:00 draft your assigned section of an initiation (competitive landscape, 6 pages) · 17:30 results arrive — actuals into the model · 18:30 earnings call, typing fast · 20:00 send the variance table and draft note to your lead · 22:00 note filed with compliance for morning release.

## The documents you would produce
The living model (Excel) · preview, results, and update notes · the initiation · channel-check trackers · estimate tables. When you read "the company beat consensus by 3%," you are reading the averaged output of desks like this.

## Who pays
Historically, trading commissions. Increasingly, direct research payments. The money in this business is shrinking worldwide. That is exactly why juniors who can *automate* the routine work are valued. A Python script that reads results and builds the variance table in minutes — your Appendix A skill — is a real edge.

## The India angle
Research analysts are regulated by **SEBI's Research Analyst Regulations**: registration, an exam (NISM Series XV), disclosure of your own holdings, and a wall between research and investment banking. Results season in India is packed — hundreds of companies report in the same two weeks. Also: a large share of global research support work is done from **GCCs in India**. Many careers in this field start in Bengaluru or Gurgaon, supporting an analyst in New York. It is a real and underrated entry door.

## Your course, in miniature
The model = Modules 3+5 on company_financials.csv · the variance table = 5A exactly · the valuation = 8B's football field · the results note = 12.5's one-page memo, written under time pressure · the channel-check habit = Module 1's provenance questions, asked of people instead of files.

**Try this (one evening):** take MoneyMart's FY24-25 from company_financials.csv. Pretend FY25-26 results just came in 5% below your revenue expectation. Write a two-page "results note": headline view, what changed, revised outlook, one chart. Done well, this is an equity-research interview passed.

# D.2 — Investment Banking Execution: From "Mandate" to "Deal Closed"

**The job in one line:** run the process that sells a company or raises its capital. Part project manager, part analyst, part document factory — under the highest deadline pressure in finance.

**The people:** the bank's deal team. The MD wins the client and manages the relationship. The VP or associate runs the process. Analysts build everything. Around them: the buyer's team, lawyers on both sides, the due diligence firms (section D.3), and the client company's very tired CFO.

## The process — selling a company, start to finish

**Station 0 · The pitch (before any deal exists).** Banks compete for the job using **pitchbooks**: 40–100 slides. Industry context, "your strategic options," a rough valuation, and why this bank. Analysts build these at night. Most pitches lose. The winning one becomes months of work. The pitchbook is where 8B's comps and football field appear as a *sales document*.

**Station 1 · Preparation (weeks 1–4).** The mandate is signed. Now build:
- **The model**: a full financial model of the client, built with the CFO's numbers. It is the deal's single source of truth.
- **The teaser**: 2 pages, no company name ("a leading South-based specialty retailer"). Sent widely to test interest.
- **The CIM** (Confidential Information Memorandum): 60–120 pages. The company's full story. Sent only to buyers who sign confidentiality agreements (NDAs). Analysts write it and rewrite it.
- **The buyer list**: strategic buyers (companies) and financial buyers (private equity funds), ranked by likelihood.

**Station 2 · Marketing (weeks 4–10).** Teasers go out → NDAs come back → CIMs go out → management presentations happen (the CFO tells the story; you built the slides) → **non-binding bids** arrive: "We value the company at roughly ₹800–900 crore, subject to due diligence."

**Station 3 · Due diligence (weeks 10–20).** Shortlisted bidders enter the **virtual data room (VDR)** — a secure website holding *everything*: financials, contracts, court cases, tax, HR. The analyst's quiet power role: **running the data room and the Q&A tracker**. Hundreds of buyer questions arrive. Each one is logged, sent to the client, and answered in writing. (What buyers *do* with all this is section D.3.) Deal discipline lives or dies here. So do analyst weekends.

**Station 4 · Bids and negotiation (weeks 20–26).** Final binding bids arrive, with marked-up contracts. The bank keeps the pressure on ("we have stronger offers"). Lawyers fight over the **SPA** (sale and purchase agreement): price, promises, protections. Analysts model every price scenario at 2 am: "if the working-capital adjustment lands at X, the seller receives Y."

**Station 5 · Signing → closing.** Signing is the public announcement. **Closing** waits for approvals — in India usually the **CCI** (competition regulator), sometimes RBI or SEBI depending on the sector. Weeks to months. Then the deal announcement, the league-table credit, and the fee — usually 1–2% of the deal value, paid only on success. That "only on success" explains every all-nighter above.

*(IPO deals — like the Zomato, LIC, and Tata Tech examples from Module 8 — follow a cousin process: draft the prospectus with lawyers, answer SEBI's questions, do the roadshow, build the order book, set the price, list.)*

## A Thursday on a live deal (analyst)

8:30 Q&A tracker: 14 new buyer questions overnight; send 9 to the client, draft answers for 5 · 10:00 update the model with last month's actuals; rebuild the price bridge · 12:00 VP review — 40 comments on your presentation deck · 15:00 call the client's finance team to chase missing data-room files · 18:00 fix the deck · 21:00 update the bid-comparison table for tomorrow's client call · 23:30 one bidder's advisor emails 31 more questions. Log them. Go home.

## The documents
Pitchbooks · teaser · CIM · management presentation · the model · buyer list and tracker · Q&A log · bid comparison tables · closing checklists. Notice something: **half the outputs are documents, not spreadsheets.** IB analysts become very good at producing finished, error-free pages, fast. Module 12.5's exhibit discipline is survival here, not decoration.

## Who pays
The client, and only on success. High fees fund the machine. League tables (rankings by deals done) are the marketing that wins the next pitch.

## The India angle
Approval timelines (especially CCI) shape deal calendars · many Indian companies are promoter-led, so the "seller" is often a family with emotions, not just a board — half the job is managing people · the market has global banks, strong Indian houses, and boutiques. Boutiques give juniors far more deal exposure per year.

## Your course, in miniature
The model = Modules 3+5+6 on one company · the valuation = 8B exactly · the bid table = 4B's comparison exhibits · the Q&A tracker = Module 1's record-keeping discipline · the CIM = 12.5's three-rooms problem: one document, many audiences.

**Try this:** write MoneyMart's two-page anonymised teaser. The business, the numbers that sell (FY21-25 growth, store economics), the growth story — no company name. Then one paragraph: three strategic buyers and two financial buyers, with one line each on *why them*. This is a real IB interview question, answered.

# D.3 — Due Diligence: The People Who Check If the Story Is True

**The job in one line:** before someone pays ₹900 crore for a company, spend six weeks proving — or disproving — that the company is what the sellers claim.

**The people:** the buyer hires specialist teams. **Financial DD** (usually a Big 4 firm's deals team). **Commercial DD** (strategy consultants). **Legal DD** (law firms). **Tax DD**. And increasingly, **technology and data DD**. Each team writes a report. Together, the reports decide whether the deal happens, at what price, and with what protections. If D.2 is the seller's process, D.3 is the buyer's counter-process. Same data room, opposite mission.

## The process — financial DD, the analytical core

**Station 1 · Scope and the first data pull.** The contract defines the questions: "check that FY23-25 earnings are real; assess working capital; find hidden liabilities." Then the data room opens. The team pulls audited statements, monthly management reports (MIS), account records, big customer invoices, contracts, bank statements. Day one is Module 1, word for word: *what is one row here? who made this file? do the totals match across sources?* If the MIS revenue does not match the audited revenue, that is not a formatting issue. That is finding number one.

**Station 2 · Quality of Earnings (QoE) — the heart of the craft.** The question: **is the reported EBITDA real, and will it repeat?** The team rebuilds an "Adjusted EBITDA" by removing distortions:
- one-time gains dressed up as normal business (a land sale hiding inside "other income")
- costs not yet admitted (the receivables ageing shows customers are not paying; the P&L pretends they will)
- related-party games (rent paid to the promoter's other company at half the market rate — that cost will jump after the deal)
- channel stuffing (a sales spike in Q4 and a returns spike in Q1 = borrowed revenue)
- costs pushed to the balance sheet that belong in the P&L
The output is a bridge: *Reported EBITDA ₹120 cr → Adjusted ₹96 cr*. Every step of that bridge is a negotiation weapon. At a price of 10× EBITDA, that one finding just moved the price by ₹240 crore. **QoE is notebook 5A's decomposition discipline, pointed at accounting instead of operations — with a suspicious eye.**

**Station 3 · The working-capital and hidden-debt sweep.** Two deal-mechanics jobs. First, normal working capital: the contract adjusts the price if working capital is unusual. (Remember the D.2 analyst modelling this at 2 am? This is where that number comes from.) Second, the **hidden-debt hunt**: unpaid bonuses, disputed tax demands, lease obligations, customer advances. Anything that behaves like debt but hides somewhere else. Every item found = rupees off the price.

**Station 4 · Commercial DD, running in parallel.** A different firm asks a different question: *is the market story true?* They rebuild the market size from the bottom up. They do 30–50 interviews with customers, competitors and experts ("would you still buy from them at a 10% higher price?"). They run churn and cohort analysis on the customer file — literally your 5B notebook, on the target's data. Then they judge whether the CIM's hockey-stick projections survive the evidence.

**Station 5 · Red flags, the report, the decision.** Everything lands in a findings report, in three buckets: **deal-breakers** (signs of fraud, large unrecorded liabilities), **price-reducers** (the QoE bridge, hidden debt), and **fix-after-buying items** (weak controls, dependence on one key person). The report's summary is one page, answer-first — the buyer's committee reads it in the shape 12.5 taught you. Then the buyer bids lower, demands protections, or walks away.

## A Wednesday, week 3 of a QoE (associate)

9:00 reconciliation work: March MIS vs the account records show a ₹3.2 cr gap — trace it (a timing issue; document it) · 11:30 receivables ageing: the 90-days-overdue bucket doubled in a year — draft the adjustment · 14:00 management Q&A call: ask about the related-party rent; note the CFO's pause · 16:00 update the EBITDA bridge slides · 18:00 team review: the partner changes two of your adjustments, removes one, adds a new question (why did margin jump only in March?) · 19:30 log 12 new questions into the tracker. You are the buyer-side twin of D.2's analyst.

## The documents
The QoE report with its EBITDA bridge · working-capital and net-debt schedules · the red-flag register · Q&A logs · the commercial team's market model and interview summary. Every document follows one rule: a claim, its source document, its rupee impact. This is Trust Report discipline, used professionally.

## Who pays
The buyer. (Sometimes the seller runs "vendor DD" first, to get ahead of problems.) Fees are based on time, not deal success. This keeps DD's incentives clean: they are paid to find problems, not to close deals.

## The India angle
Promoter-run companies make related-party analysis the richest hunting ground · GST and e-invoicing data give Indian DD teams a checking tool most countries lack — revenue can be verified against tax filings · the Big 4's India deals teams hire many freshers. This is one of the most accessible doors into deal work. Two years of QoE teaches more real accounting than any classroom.

## Your course, in miniature
Station 1 = Module 1's Trust Report, exactly · QoE = 5A's decomposition with maximum suspicion · the receivables and cohort work = 3C+5B on their records · the red-flag register = your bias-check ritual, made industrial · the one-page summary = 12.5's memo.

**Try this:** MoneyMart's FY20-21 restatement (₹3,511 cr → ₹3,366 cr) sits in your dataset. Write the DD finding the way a professional would: what was restated, the rupee impact, two possible explanations, the three documents you would demand, and the effect on a 10× EBITDA price. Five sentences plus a small bridge. That is a deals-team interview artifact.

# D.4 — Buy-Side Investment Analysis: How a Fund Actually Decides

**The job in one line:** decide — with real money and your name attached — what a fund buys, holds, and sells.

**The people:** the fund's investment team. The portfolio manager (PM) decides. Analysts recommend. The risk team sets limits. Dealers execute the trades. The fund could be a mutual fund, PMS, AIF, insurer, or family office. The skeleton below is common to all.

## The process — from universe to position

**Station 1 · The screen.** Nobody reads 5,000 companies. Filters cut the universe to a shortlist: minimum liquidity, quality measures (return on capital, low debt), valuation bands, price momentum — whatever fits the fund's style. This is a pandas job, literally. Your 3C groupby-and-filter on a fundamentals table. The screen does not make decisions. It makes *reading lists*.

**Station 2 · The thesis.** Before deep work begins, the analyst writes the thesis in three or four sentences: *what do we believe that the market does not, why does that gap exist, and what will close it?* No thesis, no model. A model without a thesis is 6B's overfitting trap in a suit — numbers arranged to confirm a feeling.

**Station 3 · The work.** Now the full toolkit from D.1, done privately. Build or adapt the model. Read five years of annual reports and earnings-call transcripts. Do channel checks. Meet the management. Stress the unit economics. Buy-side depth is different from sell-side breadth: one analyst, 30–60 names known *cold*, nothing published. The only reader is the PM. The only grade is the fund's returns.

**Station 4 · The IC memo and the decision.** All the work compresses into an **investment committee (IC) memo**. Here is the striking part: it is *exactly* your Module 12.5 one-page template. The recommendation first, with target and confidence · the thesis in three sentences · three supporting findings with exhibits · the valuation range stated as "we would be surprised outside…" · **the pre-mortem**: what kills this idea, and what evidence would make us exit · the ask (position size). The committee attacks it. Ideas that survive become positions.

**Station 5 · Sizing and risk.** How much to buy? Conviction × liquidity × correlation with what the fund already holds × the mandate's limits (maximum single-stock %, sector caps — Module 12's constraints-as-humility, enforced by compliance software). Sizing is where 10B's distribution thinking lives. The question is not "will it go up?" It is "what does the portfolio look like in the world where it doesn't?"

**Station 6 · Monitoring and the sell discipline.** The unglamorous majority of the job. Every position's thesis is tracked against new evidence. Each quarter's results either support the thesis or wear it down. The professional's edge is the **sell discipline**: exit when the thesis breaks (which is not the same as the price falling), when the target is reached, or when a better use of the money appears. The pre-written "what would change our mind" from Station 4 is the anti-ego device — 12.5's Structure 4, with crores attached.

## A Monday (buy-side analyst, mid-cap fund)

8:15 portfolio dashboard plus overnight news on all holdings · 9:30 one holding is down 6% on a competitor's launch — pull the thesis note; is this noise or damage? Write three lines to the PM · 11:00 update the results tracker for this week's five reporting companies · 14:00 deep work: hour three on the new idea's model and call transcripts · 16:30 IC prep — your memo is up on Thursday; rehearse answers to the pre-mortem questions · 18:00 log everything into the research system. A fund's memory is these notes.

## The documents
Screens · thesis notes · models · IC memos · monitoring dashboards · the post-mortem file. Good funds formally review losers *and* winners: was the win skill or luck? That is Module 6's evaluation honesty, applied to yourself.

## Who pays
The fund's investors, through fees on assets under management (AUM). This is why the industry chases AUM, and why performance against the benchmark is the life-or-death metric. Beat the index, or you are overhead. That is Module 6's naive floor, with a marketing budget.

## The India angle
SEBI's fund categories make mandates unusually strict (a "large-cap fund" *must* hold 80% large caps — the constraints are law) · the SIP flood means analysts increasingly think about money flows, not just fundamentals · monthly portfolio disclosure means you can *study any Indian fund's real decisions*. An underused trick: pick one fund, read twelve months of its portfolio changes, and reverse-engineer its theses.

## Your course, in miniature
The screen = 3C · the thesis-versus-evidence loop = Module 5's mechanisms and tests · the model = Modules 3+5+6 · sizing = Modules 10+12 · the IC memo = 12.5's template, word for word · the sell discipline = the pre-commitment habit. Track A of your capstone, presented as an IC memo, *is* this job's audition.

**Try this:** pick any listed Indian company you know as a customer. Write only Station 2 — the four-sentence thesis: what you believe, why the market disagrees, what will close the gap, and what would prove you wrong. Most people cannot do this in four sentences. Learning to is the buy-side's core skill.

# D.5 — Derivatives Research & Strategy: The Professional Side of India's Loudest Market

**The job in one line:** analyse options and futures — positioning, pricing, risk — to build strategies and hedges, in the world's largest derivatives market by contracts: the NSE.

**Why this section exists:** your generation talks about options constantly. Almost all of that talk is the retail-gambling version. The professional version is a different job. It is less about predicting direction. It is more about **measuring what the market believes, and finding where those beliefs are wrongly priced — or worth insuring against**. The contrast between the two versions is itself the lesson.

**The people:** derivatives strategists at brokerages (they publish for clients). Desk traders and quants at prop firms and market makers (11A's world). Risk teams that price exposure. And advisors who help companies hedge real business risks — an exporter's dollar income, for example. That last one is Module 8's corporate stream meeting this one.

## The process — what the work actually is

**Station 1 · Read the market's positioning, every morning.** The desk rebuilds the picture daily from exchange data. **Open interest (OI)** changes by strike: where are positions building up? The futures **basis** (premium or discount to the spot price): a stress signal. **Put-call ratios**. Foreign and domestic investor positions from exchange disclosures. Rollover behaviour near expiry. The output is a positioning read: "Heavy call OI at 25,000 is acting as a ceiling. Puts are building at 24,000. The market is pricing a range." This is descriptive analytics — Module 4 — on a dataset most people never look past.

**Station 2 · Read what volatility is saying.** The professional's core object is not price. It is **implied volatility (IV)**: the amount of uncertainty the market has priced into option premiums. The desk tracks four things. IV versus its own history (is it high or low, in percentile terms?). IV versus *realised* volatility — your 9B and 10C machinery: is the market paying too much or too little for insurance? The **skew**: do downside puts cost more than upside calls? Fear is measurable. And the **term structure**: is next week's big event already priced in? One sentence to remember: **option markets trade volatility the way stock markets trade price.** Direction is mostly the amateur layer.

**Station 3 · Build the strategy.** Given a view — on direction, on a range, or purely on volatility — the strategist assembles it from standard parts. Spreads (limited risk). Straddles and strangles (bets on volatility). Condors (bets on a range). Calendars (bets on the term structure). Collars (a cheap hedge for someone holding a stock). The craft is matching *view → structure → payoff diagram → maximum loss*. And the professional rule: **maximum loss is decided before entry.** Position size comes from 10C's tail thinking, not from hope. A published note reads like: "Nifty weekly: IV at the 78th percentile before the event. We favour selling the 24,800–25,200 strangle, protected beyond 300 points. Maximum loss ₹X per lot."

**Station 4 · The Greeks — managing the risk, live.** Once positions exist, the job becomes managing sensitivities. They are known by Greek letters. **Delta**: exposure to direction (hedged continuously). **Gamma**: how fast delta changes (the reason expiry days are wild). **Theta**: time decay (the seller's income, the buyer's slow bleed). **Vega**: exposure to volatility (the professional's real position). A market-making desk — 11A's maker, upgraded — is basically a machine that stays delta-neutral, earns spread and theta, and survives gamma and vega storms. Awareness level is enough here. The point: **the risk has many dimensions**, and desks watch the Greeks the way pilots watch instruments.

**Station 5 · The event playbook and the post-mortem.** Budget day. RBI policy. Election results. Earnings. Events are where IV, positioning and payoffs collide. Desks prepare playbooks in advance: "if the policy is a surprise cut, unwind X; the skew will move like Y." Afterwards, honest desks run the 6B ritual: did we earn because our view was right, or because everything went up? Profit is attributed by Greek: "this month we made money on theta and lost on vega." That is the evaluation harness, professionalised.

## A Thursday (expiry day, derivatives strategist at a broker)

8:00 read overnight global markets and volatility · 8:40 the morning derivatives note goes out: OI map, IV percentile, expected expiry range · 9:15 market opens — watch the wild behaviour around the biggest-OI strikes · 11:00 client call: a PMS wants a collar on a concentrated holding; build three versions with costs · 14:00 next week's RBI event note: term-structure chart, three scenario payoffs · 15:30 expiry settles; log this week's calls against outcomes, honestly · 17:00 draft the weekly strategy publication.

## The documents
Daily positioning notes · IV dashboards and percentile trackers · strategy notes with payoff diagrams and a stated maximum loss · hedging proposals for companies and funds · post-event attribution reports.

## Who pays
Brokerage clients (research comes bundled with execution). Prop desks (their own profits). Companies (hedging advice). Funds (overlay strategies). The retail boom made derivatives *content* a business of its own. That is exactly why SEBI's risk warnings exist. The famous statistic — that the large majority of retail F&O traders lose money — is a regulatory disclosure, not an opinion. Professional desks are mostly on the other side of those trades. They are 11A's house.

## The India angle
NSE's weekly index options are the most traded derivative contracts in the world — the liquidity, and the career demand, are unmatched · SEBI has been actively tightening the retail derivatives rules (lot sizes, expiry schedules, disclosures) — knowing the rulebook is a job skill here · and corporate India's real hedging needs (currency above all — the exporter and importer story) make the *advisory* side a stable, less glamorous, very employable niche.

## Your course, in miniature
The positioning read = Module 4's chart grammar on exchange data · IV versus realised = 9B + 10C's volatility machinery — the one course thread that runs straight onto a derivatives desk · payoff structuring = 7B's decision trees, drawn as diagrams · the max-loss rule = 10C's tail thinking · the maker's inventory = 11A exactly · the post-mortem = 6B's harness. One honest boundary: actual option *pricing* (Black-Scholes and its family) is the next course's mathematics. This course gave you the volatility half — which is the half desks argue about.

**Try this:** compute NIFTY's realised volatility from your course dataset (10C's one-line calculation). Then look up today's India VIX — the market's implied volatility. Write three sentences: which is higher, what that gap means in plain words ("the market is paying more for insurance than recent weather justifies — or is not"), and what event on the calendar might explain it. That is a real contribution to a derivatives desk's morning meeting.

# D.6 — Credit Research & Ratings: The People Behind "AA−, Outlook Stable"

**The job in one line:** answer one question — *will this borrower pay back, on time, in full?* — and publish the answer as a grade the whole financial system prices against.

**Why this section closes the appendix:** in Module 8's IL&FS case, you met a rating **failing** — AAA just weeks before default. This section shows how the grade is *made*. That is also the honest map of where it can break. And it is one of India's largest analyst employers: CRISIL, ICRA, CARE, India Ratings — plus every bank's internal credit team, and every debt fund's own credit analysts.

**The people:** rating-agency analysts (the public graders). Bank credit teams (grading their own borrowers — Module 8's banking stream). Buy-side credit analysts (funds that hold bonds, running D.4's process with a debt lens).

## The process — how a rating gets made

**Station 1 · The mandate and the pack.** The borrower (the *issuer*) requests a rating — and pays for it. Hold that thought. The analyst receives the pack: 3–5 years of financials, projections, bank statements, order books, and access to management. The first hour is Module 1: reconcile the numbers, check restatements as-they-were-reported (a company that revises its history is telling you something), and map the group structure. In India, **the group is the unit of risk**. Promoter cross-holdings, loans between group companies, and guarantees mean a healthy company inside a sick group is not healthy.

**Station 2 · The business risk read.** Before any ratio: how survivable is the business itself? Industry cycles. Competitive position. Customer concentration — one customer giving 40% of revenue is a rating fact. Regulatory exposure. This is Module 5's diagnostic instinct: what *drives* these cash flows, and what breaks them?

**Station 3 · The financial risk read — the ratio engine.** Now the numbers. Unlike equity work, credit has a fixed canon. **Leverage** (Debt ÷ EBITDA). **Coverage** (EBITDA ÷ interest — the single most-watched number: how many times over can they pay the interest?). **Gearing** (Debt ÷ Equity). Cash-flow adequacy (cash generated versus debt coming due). And **liquidity** — the killer category. IL&FS's core lesson lives here: *running out of value and running out of cash are different deaths.* A company can own plenty of assets and still default, because ₹500 crore falls due on Tuesday and nobody will refinance it this week. Credit analysts build **debt maturity ladders** — a chart of what is due, when — and stress them. That is 9C's cone thinking, pointed at repayments.

**Station 4 · Projections under punishment.** The issuer's projections are received politely — and then stress-tested hard. Cut the revenue. Squeeze the margins. Refinance at 2% higher cost. Remove the biggest customer. The question is never "what does management expect?" It is: "in the *bad-but-plausible* world, does coverage stay above 1?" That is your 10B honesty, with default as the tail.

**Station 5 · Committee, grade, and the watch.** The analyst presents to a **rating committee**. The committee exists on purpose: no single analyst — who can be befriended or pressured — owns the grade. The output: the letter grade (AAA down to D; "investment grade" versus below), the **outlook** (stable, positive, negative — the direction of travel), and a published **rationale**. Its shape is 12.5's pyramid: the grade first, three drivers, the triggers. Then **surveillance**, forever: quarterly monitoring, covenant tracking, and the actions everyone reads — downgrade, upgrade, watch. The system's honest weakness, said plainly: **the issuer pays for its own grade.** The industry manages this conflict with committees, fixed criteria, and regulation — and it failed, visibly, in IL&FS. Being able to state that conflict *and* its safeguards calmly is a credit-interview distinguisher.

**Station 6 · The bank's twin.** Inside banks, the same skeleton runs privately. Every large loan gets a **credit appraisal memo**: business read, ratio engine, stress test, collateral, recommendation. Then portfolio monitoring — where your Module 5 vintage analysis and Module 3's early-warning flags (missed payments, rising credit usage, bounced cheques) are the daily machinery. RBI's rules make this the most regulation-shaped analysis job in India.

## The documents

## Who pays
Issuers pay the agencies (the managed conflict above). Banks pay their own credit teams. Fund investors pay for buy-side credit work. Demand is steady in every market cycle. Someone must always answer: *will they pay?*
